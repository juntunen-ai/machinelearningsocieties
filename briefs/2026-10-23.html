The proof got cheap; the specification became the scarce good

Signal 4/10 · Stakes 5/10

On 22 October Anthropic reported that Claude, working with the verification firm Theorem, had written machine-checked proofs that 239 entry points across 123 open-source libraries cannot crash, under stated assumptions, on any input up to 64 MB. The proofs run to about 180 million lines of Rocq. Verification stops being a boutique service and becomes a step bought by the token; what stays scarce is the specification the proof is checked against.

Signal

The Frontier Red Team published the work with Theorem on 22 October. The pipeline compiles each library to RISC-V, applies Theorem's formal semantics for the instruction set and for Linux process behaviour, and has Claude write the proof; the Rocq kernel then checks it mechanically. Anthropic says Claude also patched the bugs it found, including defects that had survived years of fuzzing and, by its account, trillions of test runs. A subset of proofs, the RISC-V and operating-system semantics and a Claude skill are published, and Theorem offers to run the pipeline for other projects.

Observed. The numbers come from the primary post: 239 entry points, 123 OSS-Fuzz libraries, about 180 million lines of Rocq, a typical proof of roughly 600,000 lines for a binary of 18,000 RISC-V instructions, inputs bounded at 64 MB. The proofs assume a single-threaded static binary, a fixed memory budget, a RISC-V processor and a modelled Linux.

Caveats. Deployed reality differs: most of these libraries ship on x86 or ARM and are dynamically linked, so the same code carries no guarantee there. The proofs cover named entry points, not whole libraries. Generation consumes hundreds of millions of tokens, which prices the method for well-resourced users. And every line is only as good as the trusted base, the machine models and the exact theorem statements; a defect there invalidates 180 million lines at once.

Assessment. The engineering claim is narrower than the headline and more useful than it looks. What changed is the ratio: proof labour used to dominate, now the binding cost is the specification and the trusted semantics. That moves the assurance question from whether a human reviewed the code to what exactly was proved, against which model of the machine. The stakes are a workflow change across a sector, not yet a rule change.

Three plus zero plus one is four out of ten.

Stakes 5/10. Machine-checked assurance becomes a repeatable workflow across software producers, one step below a procurement rule naming it as required evidence.

Thesis

When proof generation is priced by tokens, the scarce good is the specification. Whoever writes the spec and the model of the machine writes the standard of care, because a proof only proves the spec. Diffusion of AI into software production will show up first in assurance contracts, in what counts as evidence that a component is safe, rather than in faster code generation.

Antithesis

Proofs prove what the spec says, and here the same pipeline writes both, so verification can launder wrong assumptions into a certificate. The trusted base is one collaborator's model of RISC-V and Linux, a single point of failure that no court will audit. The assumptions exclude the machines actually in use. Formal verification has promised more than it delivered for four decades, and the constraint may simply move to specifications nobody has time to write.

Forecast

By 31 October 2027, at least one European harmonised standard, ENISA guidance or national public-procurement criterion will name machine-checked proofs as acceptable assurance evidence for open-source components (55 %). It resolves on a published document that names proofs as evidence, and fails if none appears by that date.

Political consequences

In one to two years, the instrument is the Cyber Resilience Act's harmonised standards and the procurement criteria that follow them; the actor is the Commission with ENISA and the national market-surveillance authorities. The observable is a standard or tender criterion listing formal proof among accepted evidence, or an explicit refusal to accept it.

In three to four years, the instrument is liability: the revised Product Liability Directive applies from December 2026, and the question is whether a machine-checked proof rebuts a claim of defect. The actors are courts and the Commission's guidance; the observable is the first judgment or official guidance treating a proof as conformity evidence.

Market test: pro-market. The instrument is publication, a subset of proofs, the machine semantics and a Claude skill any maintainer can use; the actors are Anthropic and Theorem. The gain goes to new entrants into assurance and to maintainers who could never afford a proof team. The caveat is concentration: if the trusted semantics stay one firm's, the entry that opens today closes tomorrow.

What to watch

Whether an independent team reproduces one of the published proofs against a different model of the machine. That, not another announcement, is what turns a demonstration into accepted evidence.
