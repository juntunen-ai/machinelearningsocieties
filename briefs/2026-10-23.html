The proof got cheap; the specification became the scarce good

Signal 4/10 · Stakes 5/10

On 22 October Anthropic reported that Claude, working with the verification firm Theorem, had written machine-checked proofs that 239 functions in 123 open-source libraries cannot crash, under stated assumptions, on any input up to 64 megabytes. A proof of this kind is not a test. A test tries inputs and hopes to hit the failure; a proof is a mathematical argument that a program checks line by line, and if the checker accepts it, the code meets the stated claim for every input in the allowed range. The proofs come to about 180 million lines of Rocq, a language built for writing proofs a computer can verify (it was long known as Coq). Verification stops being a boutique service bought by the hour and becomes a step bought by the token. What stays scarce is the specification: the sentence that says exactly what was proved.

Signal

The Frontier Red Team published the work with Theorem on 22 October. The pipeline compiles each library for RISC-V — an open processor instruction set that anyone may implement, unlike the x86 in most laptops or the ARM in most phones — and applies Theorem's formal semantics: an exact mathematical description of what each instruction and each Linux system call does. Claude writes the proof; the Rocq kernel, a small program that accepts only valid proofs, checks it mechanically. Anthropic says Claude also patched the defects it found, including some that had survived years of fuzzing — automated random-input testing — and, by its account, trillions of test runs. A subset of the proofs, the RISC-V and operating-system semantics and a Claude skill for the task are public, and Theorem offers to run the pipeline for other projects.

Observed. The numbers come from the primary post: 239 entry points (functions that code outside the library can call), 123 OSS-Fuzz libraries, about 180 million lines of Rocq, a typical proof of some 600,000 lines for a binary of 18,000 RISC-V instructions, and inputs capped at 64 megabytes. The proofs assume a single-threaded static binary — one thing running at a time, with all code fixed at build time — a set memory budget, a RISC-V processor and a modelled Linux.

Caveats. Deployed reality differs. Most of these libraries ship on x86 or ARM machines and are linked dynamically, loading part of their code at run time — a path the proof does not model — so the same source carries no guarantee there. The proofs cover named entry points, not whole libraries. Generating them consumes hundreds of millions of tokens, which prices the method for well-resourced users. And every line is only as good as the trusted base: the parts that must be believed without proof, namely the checker, the model of the chip and the model of the operating system. A defect there invalidates 180 million lines at once.

Assessment. The headline is narrower than it sounds and more useful than it looks. What changed is the price of the labour, not the standard of proof: a proof used to take months of a specialist's time, and now it takes tokens. The binding constraint moves to two things a machine cannot invent for you — a precise statement of what the code must do, and a trustworthy model of the machine it runs on. In practice this changes what a buyer can ask for. A procurement team can require a proof of named properties, where before it could ask only for a test report or a code review. A maintainer of a small library can obtain assurance once available only to firms with a verification team. And a certificate becomes a document that has to be read: it is worth exactly what its assumptions are worth. The stakes are a workflow change across a software sector, not yet a rule that makes a proof mandatory.

Evidence 4/10.

  • Primary source = 3: Anthropic's research post, with the numbers and assumptions stated.
  • Independent confirmation = 0: no newsletter in the inbox had covered the item when this edition was written.
  • First mover = 1: Anthropic posted the work on X before the newsletters did.

Three plus zero plus one is four out of ten.

Stakes 5/10. Machine-checked assurance becomes a repeatable workflow across software producers — one step below a procurement rule that names it as required evidence.

Thesis

When proof writing is paid for in tokens, the scarce good is the specification. A proof only proves the sentence it is handed, so whoever writes that sentence, and the model of the machine it refers to, sets the standard of care. The spread of AI into software production will therefore show up first in contracts and assurance — in what counts as evidence that a component is safe — rather than in faster code generation.

Antithesis

A proof is only as good as its statement, and here one pipeline writes both the code and the claim, so a wrong assumption can be laundered into a certificate. The trusted base is one partner's model of RISC-V and Linux: a single point of failure that no court is equipped to audit. The assumptions exclude the machines almost everyone actually runs. Formal verification has promised more than it delivered for forty years, and the constraint may simply move to specifications nobody has time to write.

Forecast

By 31 October 2027, at least one European harmonised standard — a technical standard that gives a product the presumption of conforming to EU law — ENISA guidance or a national public-procurement criterion will name machine-checked proofs as acceptable assurance evidence for open-source components (55 %). It resolves on a published document that names proofs as evidence, and fails if none appears by that date.

Political consequences

In one to two years, the instrument is the Cyber Resilience Act — the EU law that places security requirements on products with digital elements — and the harmonised standards and procurement criteria that follow it. The actor is the Commission with ENISA, the EU cybersecurity agency, and the national market-surveillance authorities. The observable is a standard or tender criterion that lists formal proof among accepted evidence, or one that explicitly refuses it.

In three to four years, the instrument is liability. The revised Product Liability Directive applies from December 2026, and the open question is whether a machine-checked proof rebuts a claim that a product was defective. The actors are courts and the Commission's guidance; the observable is the first judgment or official guidance that treats a proof as conformity evidence.

Market test: pro-market. The instrument is publication — a subset of the proofs, the machine semantics and a Claude skill any maintainer can use; the actors are Anthropic and Theorem. The gain goes to new entrants into assurance and to maintainers who could never afford a proof team. The caveat is concentration: if the trusted semantics stay one firm's, the entry that opens today closes tomorrow.

What to watch

Whether an independent team reproduces one of the published proofs against a different model of the machine. That, not another announcement, is what turns a demonstration into accepted evidence.

A watch item is not a forecast. It names the document, decision or number that would settle the question this edition raises, so the call can be checked when it appears rather than argued about afterwards.

How the signal is scored

Two numbers are given for every edition, and they are read together. The first measures how good the evidence is. The second measures how much changes if the evidence holds.

Signal, 0–10: the evidence. Written as E = 3P + N + L and capped at ten.

  • P — primary source. Three points when the claim rests on the actor's own document, filing or statement. Zero when it rests only on reporting about the actor.
  • N — independent confirmation. One point for each additional newsletter or outlet that adds a fact of its own. Reprinting the same press release adds nothing.
  • L — first mover. One point when the party at the centre of the story is the one that published it first.

A story carried by the actor's own document, confirmed by one independent outlet that adds a fact, and published first by the actor scores five. The same story repeated by ten outlets that add nothing still scores four.

Stakes, 1–10: the consequence.

  • 1 — a single team's experiment.
  • 5 — a workflow adopted across a sector.
  • 7 — a procurement rule or standard changing.
  • 10 — market structure changing.

The anchors are steps, not a scale of effort: 3 is a change inside one organisation, 9 is a rule that binds most of a market. High signal with low stakes is a well-evidenced event that does not travel. Low signal with high stakes is a thin claim about something large, and it is labelled as one.
