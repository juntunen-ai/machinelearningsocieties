1 November 2026 - Machine Learning Societies

Britain buys planning capacity with the committee's discretion

Signal 4/10 · Stakes 8/10

On 30 October the government put a national scheme of delegation into force and expanded PlanAssist, a planning tool built with Google DeepMind, Google Cloud and Faculty, from three councils to fifteen. The tool writes the first assessment of a householder application, and those applications are nearly 70 % of the total.

Signal

Observed. The Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government published the change on 30 October. From Saturday 31 October, a national scheme of delegation gives planning officers, not committees, the decision on specified categories of straightforward applications. On the same day the department expanded PlanAssist from Camden, Barnet and Dorset to fifteen councils for twelve months. The tool gathers the application, the local policy and the site constraints in one workspace. It writes an evidence-backed first assessment, and an officer edits it and decides. The government says early testing cut officer time on key planning tasks by 58 %, and that this halves routine householder decisions from eight weeks to four. The software is not mandatory, and the aim is every council in England by the end of 2027.

Caveats. The 58 % figure is the government's own, and the vendor's. It covers selected tasks in a pilot of three councils, not the whole file, and no outside body has repeated the test. The claim of eight weeks to four is an inference from task time, not an observed queue. The scheme would exist without the software. An officer keeps the decision, and a decision that follows a summary is not the same task as one that follows a file, but no published study measures the gap.

Assessment. For a reader outside AI, the state has found the unit in which it will buy the technology: the officer-minute. The saving is real to a council budget and invisible to the applicant until the queue moves. What changes is where judgement sits. A committee of elected members decides a householder application today. From 31 October an officer decides, reading a draft that a vendor's model wrote. The accountability chain still ends at the council. The reasoning now starts inside a product the council did not build and cannot inspect. Stakes are 8: a statutory scheme changed who decides nearly 70 % of applications, and one supplier group is placed inside that workflow. That is more than a procurement rule and less than a new liability rule.

Evidence 4/10.

  • P: 3 points. A primary document exists: the MHCLG release of 30 October, with the scheme, the numbers and the named councils.
  • N: 0 points. No newsletter in this morning's sample carried the item. Four newsrooms repeated the release; none added a fact of its own.
  • L: 1 point. The department published on 30 October, and Google DeepMind posted on 31 October, before the newsletters.

Three plus zero plus one is four. The evidence is one official document: strong on facts, silent on effects.

Stakes 8/10. A statutory scheme moved the decision on most applications, and one supplier group is installed in the workflow that produces it.

Thesis

The state does not buy intelligence. It buys minutes. Once the unit is officer time, the cheapest saving is to move work from the officer to the tool, and the tool's output becomes the document the decision rests on. Within two years most householder decisions in England will be an officer's signature under a summary no councillor read. The AI arrives on the same day as the delegation and inherits the discretion that the committee lost.

Antithesis

Committees were never the safeguard that the objection assumes. Most agendas follow the officer's recommendation, most applications are uncontested, and delay costs a household more than the review is worth. Delegation to professional officers is the norm in comparable systems. The officer can edit the draft, and the tool retrieves policies and site constraints that a junior officer would otherwise look up by hand. The real risk is a slower and more expensive service, not a quieter one.

Forecast

By 31 October 2027 MHCLG publishes an evaluation of the fifteen-council beta that reports the change in end-to-end decision time for householder applications, and not only officer time on tasks. Resolution date: 31.10.2027. Scoring rule: the claim hits if the department, its evaluator or an independent body publishes end-to-end decision times. It misses if the only published measure stays officer time on tasks. Probability: 60 %.

Political consequences

One to two years. The instrument is the National Scheme of Delegation, in force since 31 October. The actor is MHCLG, with the fifteen councils. The observable is the delegated-decision share in the quarterly planning application statistics for England: if officers decide most householder cases, the committee's discretion has gone before any evaluation reports.

Three to four years. The instrument is the duty to publish planning documents as open data, promised for the end of 2027, and the council contracts that follow the beta. The actors are councils as contracting authorities, under the Procurement Act 2023. The observable is the first tender for an AI assessment tool that names a vendor, or one that stays vendor-neutral.

Market test: mixed, pro-business dominant. The instrument is the Incubator for AI route that builds the tool with named partners and then offers it to councils at no charge; the open-data duty is the pro-market counterweight. The named partners gain a reference deployment across England that a new entrant cannot match next quarter, and that effect lands first.

What to watch

Does MHCLG publish end-to-end decision times? Does a council procurement name a vendor? Does the delegated-decision share jump in the next quarterly statistics? Does an appeal argue that the officer's report was machine-drafted?
