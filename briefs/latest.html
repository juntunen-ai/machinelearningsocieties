29 October 2026 - Machine Learning Societies

Google put its strongest model behind a guest list, and the guest list is now the policy

Signal 7/10 · Stakes 7/10

Google released Gemini 4 Carbon, its strongest model, to a small group of vetted cyber defenders on 28 October. The company published no admission test, no model identifier and no date for general access. Frontier capability is now allocated by permission, not by price.

Signal

Observed. Google DeepMind announced Gemini 4 Carbon on 28 October as its strongest model, with state-of-the-art results in cybersecurity and in long tasks. Carbon ranks first on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index version 5.0 with 59 points; Claude Opus 5.5 (max) follows at 58. Google reports 88.2 per cent on SEC-bench Pro, which tests the discovery of vulnerabilities; 54.8 per cent on ExploitGym, which tests exploitation; and 71.0 per cent on CWE-bench, which tests patching. Google ran the first two tests itself and measured all three without guardrails. Access starts this week for a subset of cyber defenders. Wider access follows through the Fairwind Program, Google's early-access channel for security organisations, which reports more than 650 partners. Google says it joins the voluntary process through which the United States government tests models before release. The introductory price is 2 dollars per million input tokens and 10 dollars per million output tokens, then 4 and 20 dollars.

Caveats. Most numbers come from Google. Only CWE-bench appears on an outside board, where Collinear lists Carbon at 71 per cent. Google published no safety report, model identifier or context limit. On 29 October the Fairwind page still named Gemini 4 Argon, not Carbon, and gave no date for Carbon. Google withholds the criteria for a cyber defender, so the boundary cannot be tested. One newsletter called Carbon an energy-efficiency model; the announcement makes no such claim.

Assessment. The benchmark lead is small and will move. The durable change is the door. A hospital group, a grid operator or a national cyber authority can now hold a capability that a competitor cannot buy at any price, because a vendor and a government process admitted it. For a reader outside technology, one question changes: is the firm that guards your water utility on the list? Nothing published today answers that. Stakes reach 7 because the admission rule decides who receives frontier defensive capability.

Evidence 7/10.

  • P: 3 points. A primary source exists. Google published the model and the programme rules.
  • N: 3 points. Three newsletter senders added a fact: TLDR AI, The Deep View and ben's bites.
  • L: 1 point. Google posted on 28 October, before the newsletters wrote on 29 October.

Three plus three plus one is seven. Trade coverage is broad, but most of it repeats the launch post.

Stakes 7/10. The rule that decides which defenders receive frontier capability changes, and a vendor writes it.

Thesis

Admission is becoming a main instrument of AI governance, and it is being built in private. A parliament writes a rule that binds everyone. A programme like Fairwind writes a rule that binds only the admitted, and it can change without a vote. The list becomes the licence, because a buyer that wants the best defensive tool must show that it is inside. Insurers will soon ask for that membership as evidence of diligence, and a marketing channel becomes a market requirement. The vendor also holds the information that regulators lack: who uses the capability, and for what. The result is a permission market. It can be prudent. It is not accountable.

Antithesis

The strongest objection is that gating is responsible, and the alternative is worse. A model that finds, exploits and patches vulnerabilities at this level is a dual-use tool. A public release would give every attacker the same reach. The programme has rules: background checks, named users, phishing-resistant login, no resale, defensive tasks only. Carbon also shows fewer dangerous behaviours in Google's own tests, so the delay buys time to finish guardrails. On this reading the guest list is a safety measure, and the honest criticism is only that its criteria stay unpublished. The policy is reversible: Google can open access in a month.

Forecast

By 31 March 2027, at least one European national cyber authority or national computer emergency response team will state in a public document that it holds access to Gemini 4 Carbon or to a comparable gated frontier model. Resolution date: 31 March 2027. Scoring rule: the claim resolves true if a government, agency or response-team page, annual report or press release names the model and the access. Probability: 55 per cent.

Political consequences

One to two years. The instrument is a national framework agreement, reinforced by the supply-chain rules of the NIS2 directive. The actor is a member-state cyber authority or a central purchasing body. The observable is a published framework agreement or tender that names a vendor early-access programme as a condition of supply.

Three to four years. The instrument is the general-purpose model regime of the EU AI Act, read with public procurement law. The actor is the AI Office with national supervisors. The observable is a rule that public defenders hold a second, self-hostable model, or a published allocation rule for frontier capability.

Market test: pro-business. The instruments are admission to the Fairwind Program and the voluntary pre-release testing process of the United States government. Google and its admitted partners gain. A new defender cannot compete on the same terms next quarter, because it cannot buy the capability at any price.

What to watch

Does Google publish admission criteria and a Carbon model card? Does the Fairwind page name Carbon? Does a European agency confirm access? Does a rival open a competing channel, which would show that the model is a market norm and not one vendor's choice?

How the signal is scored

Two numbers are given for every edition, and they are read together. The first measures how good the evidence is. The second measures how much changes if the evidence holds.

Signal, 0–10: the evidence. Written as E = 3P + N + L and capped at ten.

  • P — primary source. Three points when the claim rests on the actor's own document, filing or statement. Zero when it rests only on reporting about the actor.
  • N — independent confirmation. One point for each additional newsletter or outlet that adds a fact of its own. Reprinting the same press release adds nothing.
  • L — first mover. One point when the party at the centre of the story is the one that published it first.

A story carried by the actor's own document, confirmed by one independent outlet that adds a fact, and published first by the actor scores five. The same story repeated by ten outlets that add nothing still scores four.

Stakes, 1–10: the consequence.

  • 1 — a single team's experiment.
  • 5 — a workflow adopted across a sector.
  • 7 — a procurement rule or standard changing.
  • 10 — market structure changing.

The anchors are steps, not a scale of effort: 3 is a change inside one organisation, 9 is a rule that binds most of a market. High signal with low stakes is a well-evidenced event that does not travel. Low signal with high stakes is a thin claim about something large, and it is labelled as one.
