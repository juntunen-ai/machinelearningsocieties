4 November 2026 - Machine Learning Societies

Anthropic can prove which model answered, and the auditor becomes the buyer

Signal 6/10 · Stakes 6/10

Anthropic published FlashProve on 3 November. It is an open-source prototype that proves one committed model produced one reply. On a 400-billion-parameter model, one H100 proves 110 tokens a second, and a CPU checks each proof in 17 seconds. The buyer it serves is the auditor, not the consumer.

Signal

On 3 November Anthropic published a research post, a technical report and a code repository for FlashProve. The system commits to one set of model weights. It then produces a proof that a given reply came from those weights. The commitment is a fingerprint of about 6.5 megabytes, and it does not reveal the weights. A user holds the commitment, the prompt and the reply. Anyone can check the proof without the weights and without a graphics processor.

Observed. The test ran on Llama 4 Maverick, a model with 400 billion parameters that uses about 17 billion for each token. One proof covers a window of 1,024 tokens and file size is 18.78 megabytes. One H100 processor with 24 central processing units produces 110 tokens a second. A client checks a proof in 17 seconds on 24 threads, and in 21 seconds on one thread. The offline step takes 39 minutes per model and covers about 16 million requests. The code carries the Apache 2.0 licence. Anthropic states three properties in Lean, a proof assistant that checks mathematics by machine: completeness, soundness and zero knowledge. The post also lists the limits. Proofs cover integer arithmetic and greedy decoding only. The context stops at 1,024 tokens. The model architecture is not hidden. The team has done no work on side channels. A human must audit the Lean statements against their intent. The C++ verifier that a customer would run sits outside the proofs. Anthropic says that the paper, the Lean protocol and the code may not agree. The weights are not in the release, and the company plans no deployment.

Caveats. A proof costs compute. Per processor, FlashProve takes 72 to 94 times longer than vLLM, a standard inference engine, to process the same requests. That ratio decides the use case. A provider cannot prove all traffic at that price. It can prove a sample, or one high-value request, after the fact. The system also proves identity, not quality. A proof says which weights produced which tokens. It does not say that the answer is correct, safe or permitted. The prover has no formal verification, and no independent security audit exists. A provider holds both the commitment and the server, so it still chooses which requests to prove. The guarantee is real, and the sample remains a decision.

Assessment. The prototype changes one thing for a buyer outside AI. Until now a purchaser of a hosted model relied on the seller's word that the advertised model served the request. A routing service could send the request to a cheaper model, and the buyer would still see a fluent answer. FlashProve gives the buyer a check that takes 17 seconds of processor time and no trust in the seller. For a bank, a ministry or an evaluation laboratory, that is the first technical handle on a claim that audits now ask. It does not change what the buyer must do. No rule requires a proof today. The stakes are 6 because assurance already has a budget line in finance and public administration, and this prototype changes what that line can buy, not the rule that governs it.

Evidence 6/10.

  • P. The research post, the technical report and the Apache 2.0 repository are the actor's own documents. 3 points.
  • N. Two newsrooms added a fact of their own: TechCrunch reported that the lab has no deployment plan and that fingerprints could cover closed models; Help Net Security described the three artefacts and the gap between the Lean proofs and the production verifier. 2 points.
  • L. Anthropic published the post and the code before the coverage, and its account posted on X the same evening. 1 point.

The sum is 3 + 2 + 1 = 6.

Stakes 6/10. Model assurance is an established workflow with a budget in finance and public buying, and this prototype changes what that workflow can require; no rule changes today.

Thesis

Verification becomes a product, and the audit moves from reputation to arithmetic. A proof costs the provider 72 times the compute of the answer. It costs the buyer 17 seconds of processor time. That asymmetry decides the market. The seller who can produce proofs can serve a supervised buyer. The buyer who can check a proof can change supplier without trusting the seller. Verification therefore arrives first as an audit tool, and later as a feature of the service.

Antithesis

The strongest objection is that a proof answers a narrow question at a high price. Identity is not correctness. The 1,024-token window excludes most real work. At 72 to 94 times the cost, only a sample of traffic can carry a proof, and the provider picks the sample. The paper, the Lean protocol and the verifier are not yet shown to agree. Anthropic runs no deployment. A supervisor who requires proofs would also build a barrier: a large provider can afford to prove, and a small entrant cannot. On this reading the release is a research signal, and its first practical effect is a new cost of compliance.

Forecast

By 30 September 2027, at least one European public buyer or market surveillance authority names a cryptographic proof of model identity in a published tender condition, audit template or supervisory guidance (35 %). Resolution date: 30 September 2027. Scoring rule: a hit requires a published document that names the technique; a mention of assurance in general does not count.

Political consequences

One to two years. Instrument: public procurement, including the Commission's Public Procurement Act proposal of 9 September 2026 and the reporting duty in Article 33 of the proposed Cloud and AI Development Act. Actor: the Commission and national contracting authorities. Observable: one framework contract or tender that asks for a weight commitment and for proofs on a sample of requests.

Three to four years. Instrument: market surveillance and conformity assessment under the AI Act, with Article 49 registration and Article 74 supervision, and the harmonised standards of CEN-CENELEC JTC 21. Actor: the European AI Office and national market surveillance authorities. Observable: a standard or guidance that lists cryptographic attestation as evidence that the model placed on the market is the model that answered.

Market test: pro-market, narrowly. The protocol is public, the licence permits reuse, and the verifier runs on an ordinary processor. A small provider can prove parity to a buyer without revealing its weights. The cost of proving favours large providers, and that effect is real. The public interface dominates, because no one owns the check.

What to watch

Five observables. A weight commitment published for a closed frontier model. A proof overhead below ten times the cost of plain inference. One public buyer that writes a proof into a tender. An independent audit of the C++ verifier. The next update of Anthropic's Frontier Safety Roadmap, which set a prototype of provable inference as a goal for 30 September 2026.

How the Signal is scored

Every edition prints two numbers, and they are read together. The first measures the quality of the evidence. The second measures how much changes if the evidence holds.

Signal, 0–10: the evidence. The formula is E = 3P + N + L, and the maximum is ten.

  • P — primary source. Three points when the claim rests on the actor's own document, announcement or statement. Zero when it rests on reporting about the actor.
  • N — independent confirmation. One point for each newsletter or newsroom that adds a fact of its own. Repeating a press release adds nothing.
  • L — first publisher. One point when the party at the centre of the story published it first.

A story with a primary document, one independent outlet that adds a fact, and the actor as first publisher scores five. The same story repeated ten times without a new fact still scores four.

Stakes, 1–10: the consequence.

  • 1 — one team's experiment.
  • 5 — a workflow adopted across a sector.
  • 7 — a procurement rule or standard changes.
  • 10 — market structure changes.

The anchors are steps, not a scale of effort: 3 is a change inside one organisation, 9 is a rule that binds most of a market. A low signal with high stakes is a thin claim about a large thing, and it is marked as such.
